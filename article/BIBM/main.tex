\begin{abstract}
Tissue Optical Clearing (TOC) enables high-resolution 3D visualization, yet its protocol design involves multi-step physical and chemical constraints whose failure can irreversibly damage specimens. While large language models (LLMs) perform well on biomedical knowledge retrieval, their ability to generate experimentally feasible wet-lab protocols remains under-evaluated. We introduce ClearEval, a database-grounded feasibility benchmark for LLM-generated TOC protocols. ClearEval is built on a structured TOC feasibility database that integrates 372 wet-lab experimental records supporting timing intervals, 400+ curated tissue-clearing literature chunks, and expert-reviewed constraints on methods, tissues, refractive indices, fluorescent markers, and compatibility rules. Using this database, ClearEval evaluates 12 state-of-the-art LLMs through a dual-track pipeline: static knowledge is assessed by 2{,}588 multiple-choice questions, while practical protocol generation is evaluated on 253 open-ended scenarios using our Completeness, Correctness, and Effectiveness (CCE) framework. CCE is a text-reference-free metric that scores protocol viability through database-grounded rules and formulas derived from experimental confidence intervals, rather than surface similarity to reference texts. Results show that although models reach 80--91\% accuracy on static knowledge retrieval, their performance drops sharply on executable protocol generation; the strongest model falls from 91\% Knowledge Index to a 64.1 Application Index. The dominant failures arise from executable parameterization---most reliably, matching fluorescent markers to required labeling targets---rather than declarative recall, exposing a knowledge-execution gap in LLM-generated TOC protocols. Rather than making domain-general claims, ClearEval offers a depth-first, experimentally grounded feasibility check that complements breadth-oriented protocol benchmarks and provides a transferable recipe for pre-experimental evaluation in other wet-lab subdomains. Code and data: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ClearEval} (de-anonymized upon acceptance).

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Tissue Optical Clearing (TOC) renders biological tissues optically transparent
by reducing light scattering and refractive-index mismatch, enabling
non-destructive, high-resolution 3D imaging via light-sheet
microscopy~\cite{ueda2020tissue,chung2013clarity}. This capability preserves
long-range anatomical connectivity and supports high-fidelity organ-scale
atlases~\cite{wang2025advances,richardson2025unlocking}. However, TOC protocol
design is not a single-answer optimization problem. Given a specific specimen,
labeling target, imaging goal, and laboratory constraint, experimenters must
choose a feasible combination of clearing method, fluorescent marker or labeling
strategy, refractive-index condition, incubation schedule, sample geometry, and
operational constraints such as biosafety and cost. Multiple combinations may
be acceptable, but a single incompatibility---for example, a quenched
fluorophore, an unsuitable method for the tissue scale, or an unrealistic
incubation time---can invalidate the entire protocol and irreversibly damage
the specimen.

While LLMs demonstrate proficiency in biomedical question
answering~\cite{singhal2023medpalm}, existing scientific benchmarks (e.g.,
MedQA~\cite{jin2021disease}, MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}) and their recent
extensions~\cite{zhang2025benchmarking,wang2025cardbiomedbench,feng2024mediq}
predominantly assess static knowledge recall rather than experimentally
grounded protocol feasibility. Traditional semantic metrics such as
BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu} and general-purpose
evaluators~\cite{liu2023geval,li2024survey} are similarly insufficient:
a linguistically fluent protocol may still be infeasible because of an invalid
method--label combination, an inappropriate refractive-index target, or an
unrealistic incubation schedule. Such execution-level errors are subtle
wet-lab failures that generic evaluators often overlook, thereby propagating
hallucinations~\cite{bang2025hallulens,zhang2025survey}. Evaluating
LLM-generated scientific protocols therefore requires a shift from text
similarity and knowledge recall to database-grounded feasibility validation.

To bridge this gap, we introduce \textbf{ClearEval}, a database-grounded
feasibility benchmark for LLM-generated TOC protocols. ClearEval is built around
a structured TOC feasibility database that integrates 372 wet-lab experimental
records supporting timing intervals, 400+ curated tissue-clearing literature
chunks, and expert-reviewed constraints on clearing methods, tissues,
refractive-index ranges, fluorescent markers, and compatibility rules. This
database serves as the foundation for both task construction and protocol
evaluation: it defines the knowledge tested by the benchmark, the open-ended
experimental scenarios posed to the models, and the feasibility rules used to
score generated protocols.

ClearEval evaluates 12 state-of-the-art LLMs through a dual-track pipeline.
The Knowledge track measures static TOC knowledge using 2{,}588
domain-specific multiple-choice questions. The Application track evaluates
practical protocol generation on 253 open-ended scenarios using our
\textbf{CCE (Completeness, Correctness, Effectiveness) framework}. In CCE,
Completeness and Correctness assess whether the generated protocol contains the
necessary procedural elements and coherent experimental logic, while
Effectiveness uses database-grounded rules and formulas derived from
experimental confidence intervals to score executable feasibility. This design
makes ClearEval a pre-experimental feasibility gate: it does not merely ask
whether a model can recall TOC facts, but whether its generated protocol can
satisfy the physical and chemical constraints required for execution.

Our results show that high knowledge accuracy can mask execution-level failure.
For example, GPT-5.2-Fast achieves 91.1% on the Knowledge Index but only 64.1
on the Application Index (Table~\ref{tab:main_results_total}). Across models,
MCQ performance is tightly clustered, whereas open-ended protocol feasibility
varies substantially, indicating that static knowledge tasks are close to
saturation while application-level evaluation remains discriminative. The most
reliable failures arise from executable parameterization---especially matching
fluorescent markers to the required labeling targets---rather than from
declarative recall alone. These findings expose a knowledge-execution gap in
LLM-generated TOC protocols.

Our main contributions are threefold: (1) we construct a structured TOC
feasibility database that integrates wet-lab experimental records, curated
literature evidence, and expert-reviewed physical and chemical constraints into
machine-readable evaluation resources. (2) we introduce ClearEval, a
dual-track benchmark and database-grounded CCE evaluator that jointly assess
static TOC knowledge and open-ended protocol feasibility without relying on
reference-text similarity. (3) we provide an empirical analysis of 12
state-of-the-art LLMs, showing that MCQ-based knowledge evaluation is nearly
saturated whereas OEQ-based feasibility evaluation exposes model-specific
execution failures. We deliberately restrict ClearEval to the well-characterized
TOC subdomain to obtain parameter-level, experimentally grounded validation that
broad multi-subfield benchmarks cannot provide. Rather than making
domain-general claims about scientific intelligence, ClearEval offers a
depth-first feasibility benchmark and a transferable recipe for
pre-experimental evaluation in other wet-lab subdomains; external validity is
discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}.


\section{Related Works}\label{sec:related}
\noindent\textbf{Scientific LLM evaluation.} Benchmarks for scientific LLMs
have largely targeted static knowledge
retrieval~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu,jin2021disease,singhal2023medpalm} or
multidimensional reasoning~\cite{ying2025seedbench}. Generic text metrics such
as BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu} and LLM-based evaluators like
G-Eval~\cite{liu2023geval,li2024survey} reward linguistic fluency and can leave
experimentally invalid protocols undetected, masking
hallucinations~\cite{bang2025hallulens,zhang2025survey} about chemical
compatibility or incubation requirements.

\noindent\textbf{Protocol-design and wet-lab benchmarks.} Breadth-oriented
benchmarks such as BioProBench~\cite{bioprobench} (tens of thousands of
protocols across many subfields) and LAB-Bench~\cite{labbench} assess protocol
understanding largely via text/QA matching over many domains. ClearEval is
orthogonal: it trades breadth for experiment-derived, parameter-level
feasibility scoring within one subdomain, providing physics-grounded ground
truth (refractive index, incubation windows, fluorophore compatibility) that
QA-style benchmarks do not (Table~\ref{tab:positioning}).

\noindent\textbf{LLM-as-a-judge calibration.} Automated judges exhibit
documented position, verbosity, and self-preference
biases~\cite{zheng2023judge}. We therefore restrict LLM judgment to
Completeness/Correctness, ground Effectiveness in non-LLM physical checks, and
report per-dimension agreement with expert consensus (Sec.~\ref{sec:calib},
Appendix~\ref{app:judge}) rather than a single correlation.

\noindent\textbf{Scientific agents.} Autonomous-lab
systems~\cite{boiko2023coscientist,martinez2025ai} aim to close the loop from
hypothesis to experiment. ClearEval supplies a pre-experimental feasibility
gate such agents can apply before committing irreversible reagents, grounded in
the empirical parameters of Tissue Optical
Clearing~\cite{ueda2020tissue,chung2013clarity,kumar2024tissue}.

\subsection{Knowledge Base and Task Formulation}
ClearEval formalizes wet-lab constraints into a structured knowledge system,
encoded in seven curated knowledge bases covering tissue types, molecular
markers, clearing-method properties, and compatibility, derived from the
mainstream TOC literature (full inventory and provenance in
Appendix~\ref{app:curation}). To align user requirements with protocol
selection, these constraints are mapped into a 6-dimensional vector space
$\mathcal{V} \in [0,1]^6$ representing key performance indicators such as
fluorescence preservation, reagent permeability, and biosafety. Within this
space, 60 core scientific missions across various biological scales were defined
by experts to serve as seeds for realistic task generation.

\subsection{Dataset Generation Workflow}
The ClearEval pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, bottom left) instantiates
expert-authored templates against the knowledge base via controlled-vocabulary
substitution. For the Knowledge track, 45 expert templates (7 named-entity, 26
domain-knowledge, 12 task-scenario) are expanded into \textbf{2{,}588 Knowledge
Tasks (MCQs)}---533 named-entity, 1{,}350 domain-knowledge, and 705
task-scenario---each in an 8-option format to minimize correct guessing,
followed by deduplication and option shuffling. For the Application track, the
60 mission seeds are combined with the knowledge base to generate open-ended
scenarios, augmented with real wet-lab challenges and quality-filtered to
\textbf{253 Application Tasks (OEQs)}. The OEQs move beyond static reference
texts, instead presenting constrained scenarios evaluated through an automated,
experiment-based scoring pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, right). Templates,
controlled vocabularies, and filtering criteria are documented in
Appendix~\ref{app:curation}.

semantic similarity to \textbf{empirically grounded verification}, utilizing
experimental confidence intervals to evaluate the viability of generated
protocols.

\subsection{Knowledge ($I_K$) and Application ($I_A$) Indices}
The \textbf{Knowledge Index ($I_K$)} represents the macro-averaged MCQ accuracy
across domain-specific categories. To evaluate protocol design, the
\textbf{Application Index ($I_A$)} adopts a bottleneck formulation:
\begin{equation}
I_A = \min(\text{Completeness}, \text{Correctness}, \text{Effectiveness})
\end{equation}
This ``weakest-link'' approach reflects wet-lab realities, where a single
critical failure in logic or chemical compatibility can render an entire
experiment invalid.

\subsection{Empirically-Grounded Effectiveness ($E_{score}$)}
Unlike Completeness and Correctness, Effectiveness is scored by deterministic,
non-LLM checks that read directly from the expert knowledge bases
(Appendix~\ref{app:curation}). From each protocol we extract its clearing
method, target markers, refractive-index target, and total processing time, then
compute four sub-metrics whose equal-weighted sum is
$E_{score}=S_{method}+S_{label}+S_{trans}+S_{time}$ (maxima $5/6/3/3$, total
$17$).

\noindent\textbf{(1) Method suitability ($S_{method}$, max $5$).} Every clearing
method carries a six-dimensional capability vector $\mathbf{v}_{ref}\in[0,1]^6$
(fluorescence/protein preservation, dye permeability, optical clearing,
geometry, operational economy, biosafety); the scenario's requirements are
parsed into a matching demand vector $\mathbf{v}_{pred}$. $S_{method}$ rewards
their alignment by cosine similarity, and is $0$ if the proposed method is absent
from the knowledge base:
\begin{equation}
S_{method} = 5 \cdot \max\!\big(0,\ \cos(\mathbf{v}_{pred}, \mathbf{v}_{ref})\big).
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{(2) Label compatibility ($S_{label}$, max $6$).} A hard
constraint against unusable labeling, taken as the \emph{minimum} of three
$[0,6]$ rule-based checks---marker--target match, marker--fluorophore
compatibility, and fluorophore--method compatibility (the last reads the
quenching matrix). Any single incompatibility, e.g.\ a fluorophore quenched by an
organic-solvent method, drives the minimum, and hence $S_{label}$, to $0$.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Transparency validity ($S_{trans}$, max $3$).} The
refractive index the method achieves must match the tissue. We score the gap
between the tissue's native RI ($RI_{tissue}$) and the RI the method is designed
for ($RI_{ref}$) with a Gaussian of width $\sigma_{RI}$, the per-(method,
sample-scale) tolerance:
\begin{equation}
S_{trans} = 3 \cdot \exp\!\left( -\frac{(RI_{tissue}-RI_{ref})^2}{2\,\sigma_{RI}^2} \right).
\end{equation}
$S_{trans}$ is gated to $0$ when the method cannot physically handle the
requested sample scale (no method$\times$tier entry in the timing base).

\noindent\textbf{(4) Time efficiency ($S_{time}$, max $3$).} Let $\Delta t$ be
the deviation of the proposed total time from the literature-supported window
$[t_{min}, t_{max}]$ (with $\Delta t{=}0$ inside it); a Gaussian of tolerance
$\tau$ penalizes out-of-window timing:
\begin{equation}
S_{time} = 3 \cdot \exp\!\left( -\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2\,\tau^{2}} \right),
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is set deterministically to $0.2\times$ the median clearing time.
All windows, RI references, and $\sigma_{RI}/\tau$ tolerances are
literature-bounded and expert-reviewed (Appendix~\ref{app:curation}). The raw
$E_{score}$ (max $17$) is finally min--max normalized to $[0,100]$, making it
comparable with Completeness and Correctness before the $I_A$ bottleneck.

\subsection{Overall Expert Score}
To penalize models that memorize facts but fail under practical physical
constraints, the \textbf{ClearEval Expert Score} is the harmonic mean:
$\text{Score} = 2 I_K I_A / (I_K + I_A)$. Given the bottleneck formulation of
$I_A$, a high score demands both broad theoretical recall and strict
experimental compliance.

\subsection{Main Results: The Knowledge-Execution Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results_total} reveals a stark capability imbalance between
static recall and wet-lab execution: Knowledge indices cluster at $80$--$91$,
yet the bottleneck Application Index $I_A$ ranges from $64$ down to $18$. This
is visually highlighted in Fig.~\ref{fig:radar_chart}: the left panel shows all
models with uniformly high, tightly clustered Knowledge proficiency, whereas
Application Performance (right) is highly erratic. Models generally maintain
high procedural completeness ($79$ on average) but collapse on Correctness
($50$) and Effectiveness ($59$); for the three strongest models the binding
constraint is Effectiveness, for the other nine it is Correctness---though that
dimension is partly deflated by the weakly-calibrated $co\_chem$ sub-score
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}). The most reliable single failure is label suitability
($S_{label}$): models propose fluorescent markers that miss the scenario's

\subsection{Calibration of the Automated Metrics}\label{sec:calib}
We validate the automated CCE scores against blinded expert consensus on 240
protocols (20 scenarios $\times$ 12 models). Because Pearson correlation alone
ignores absolute bias, we report, per dimension, Pearson $r$, Spearman $\rho$,
ICC(2,1), quadratic-weighted $\kappa$, and Krippendorff's $\alpha$
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}; full 14-row sub-dimension breakdown in
Appendix~\ref{app:judge}). The aggregate CCE dimensions align well with experts
(ICC $0.75$/$0.65$/$0.81$ for Completeness/Correctness/Effectiveness; Overall
$r=0.84$). Crucially, agreement is \emph{uneven} across the Effectiveness
sub-metrics: the empirically-grounded label-compatibility ($S_{label}$, ICC
$0.86$) and timing ($S_{time}$, ICC $0.82$) checks align strongly with experts,
and transparency ($S_{trans}$, ICC $0.58$) moderately, whereas the
cosine-similarity method-suitability score ($S_{method}$, ICC $0.04$, $r=0.11$)
is near-degenerate---it is therefore reported as a soft preference signal rather
than a calibrated score. Among the two LLM-judged dimensions, the weakest
agreement is on chemical correctness ($co\_chem$, ICC $0.32$). We thus restrict
LLM-as-judge to Completeness and Correctness---noting that $co\_chem$ is the
least reliable sub-score and reading the corresponding failure as suggestive
(Sec.~\ref{sec:diag})---and rely on the non-LLM physical checks for
Effectiveness, where the dominant failure ($S_{label}$) is also the
best-calibrated ($\mathrm{ICC}\,0.86$).

\subsection{Fine-Grained Diagnosis: Where Execution Fails}\label{sec:diag}
Averaging normalized sub-scores over all 253 scenarios and 12 models, the
\emph{reliably}-measured deficit concentrates in label suitability
(Fig.~\ref{fig:violin_limits}). \textbf{Label suitability} ($S_{label}$, mean
$0.23$) is a hard minimum over a marker--target match and two
fluorophore-compatibility checks, and $66\%$ of protocols score $0$. Decomposing
these zeros from the scorer's own breakdown shows the binding cause is
\textbf{marker--target mismatch}---the fluorescent markers models propose do not
match the scenario's required labeling targets---rather than fluorophore
quenching, which is the minority case. Because $S_{label}$ is our best-calibrated
Effectiveness check (ICC $0.86$, Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}), this is the most
trustworthy execution failure we observe. The nominally lower chemical-safety
score ($co\_chem$, mean $0.07$) requires care: inspecting the judge's rationales
shows it is dominated by penalties for the \emph{absence} of safety/handling
caveats (fume-hood use, solvent-compatible containers) that our generation prompt
never requested; given this and its weak calibration (ICC $0.32$), we treat
$co\_chem$ as suggestive rather than a capability measure. By contrast,
transparency / refractive-index matching ($S_{trans}$, $0.64$) and timing
($S_{time}$, $0.59$) are only moderately impaired---though $S_{time}$ is
sensitive to the width of the literature time windows, and individual estimates
can still be wildly off (occasionally exceeding 800 hours against a 0--200 hour
window). The cosine method-suitability score ($S_{method}$, $0.89$) appears high
but, being near-degenerate against experts (ICC $0.04$), is discounted. In short,
the most reliable wet-lab constraint these LLMs miss is matching fluorescent
markers to the scenario's required targets.

\subsection{Inference-Time Robustness Protocol}\label{sec:baselines}
A natural follow-up question is whether the knowledge--execution gap narrows
once standard inference-time techniques are applied, rather than being an
artifact of greedy decoding. To make this directly testable on ClearEval, we
release a turnkey harness that scores four recipes against the greedy default on
the full 253-scenario Application set: (i) \textbf{few-shot} (3 in-context
exemplars); (ii) \textbf{self-consistency} ($k{=}5$ samples at temperature
$0.7$, aggregated by method-majority then continuous-field median); (iii)
\textbf{KB-RAG}, prepending retrieved knowledge-base context (method properties
and compatibility records, with numeric RI/time targets withheld so the answer
key is never leaked); and (iv) \textbf{KB-RAG $+$ self-consistency}. Because
every recipe is scored by the same calibrated CCE pipeline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}), the bottleneck Application Index $I_A$---reported with
95\% confidence intervals over samples---is directly comparable across recipes
and against Table~\ref{tab:main_results_total}. We treat KB-RAG as an upper
bound on grounding benefit and leave full literature-RAG over an external corpus
to future work. The harness and the exact run protocol are released
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}); a complete multi-model sweep under paid inference is
ongoing and will be reported in an extended version. We deliberately do not
report partial numbers here to avoid drawing conclusions from an incomplete
sweep.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
Within TOC, ClearEval shows the bottleneck is not declarative retrieval but
executable parameterization---most reliably, matching fluorescent markers to the
required labeling targets, with refractive-index and timing errors
secondary. Whether this pattern generalizes across wet-lab subdomains is an open
question our single-subdomain design cannot settle.

\noindent\textbf{External validity.} ClearEval covers one subdomain (TOC). Our
claims are therefore about TOC protocol feasibility, not LLM scientific
competence in general. Generalizing the methodology requires re-curating
subdomain-specific physical knowledge bases (refractive indices, incubation
windows, compatibility matrices), which is non-trivial
(Appendix~\ref{app:curation}); we offer CCE as a transferable recipe rather than
a finished multi-domain benchmark.

\noindent\textbf{Complementarity.} Depth-first ClearEval and breadth-first
benchmarks such as BioProBench~\cite{bioprobench} are complementary: the former
contributes experiment-derived, parameter-level feasibility scoring within a
subdomain, the latter broad coverage. Together they give a fuller picture of LLM
protocol-design ability than either alone.

\noindent\textbf{Toward grounded agents.} For LLMs to become viable in
specialized wet-lab domains, they must internalize experimental
constraints---such as reagent diffusion scales---within their reasoning loops;
ClearEval can serve as a pre-experimental feasibility gate for such agents
before irreversible reagent commitment.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} ClearEval remains a static paradigm. Real-world
wet labs involve stochastic variables (reagent degradation, tissue
heterogeneity) that are hard to model in silico; our expert grounding is
literature-bounded and refined by expert review rather than exhaustively
re-measured; and the cosine method-suitability sub-score is weakly calibrated
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}). Future work should integrate dynamic hardware feedback
so agents iteratively optimize protocols from real-time signals.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented ClearEval, an expert-curated benchmark for TOC protocol design
that shifts evaluation from semantic similarity to empirically grounded
functional validation. By leveraging a dual-track pipeline and a data-driven
CCE framework, ClearEval exposes a significant knowledge-execution gap in
current LLMs on TOC protocol design. Our results indicate that while models
excel at knowledge recall, they struggle with the marker--target matching and
physical-feasibility constraints required for wet-lab execution. We anticipate
that ClearEval---and the transferable CCE recipe---will facilitate the
development of reliable scientific agents, starting with tissue-clearing
workflows.

\section{Knowledge-Base Curation and Expert Grounding}\label{app:curation}
\noindent\textbf{Expert panel and consensus.} The knowledge bases and the CCE
rubric were curated by a panel of tissue-clearing wet-lab experts together with
a knowledge-graph specialist. Application protocols were first scored blind
against the rubric and then reconciled by panel consensus; the released scores
are per-protocol consensus values.

\noindent\textbf{Knowledge bases.} Grounding is encoded in seven JSON knowledge
bases: reference refractive index for 18 clearing methods (CUBIC, ScaleS, MACS,
iDISCO+, FDISCO, SOLID, SeeDB2, PEGASOS, BoneClear, Ce3D, TDE, ClearT2, ClearSee,
EyeCi, FOCM, SWITCH, uDISCO, FlyClear); per-method RI tolerance $\sigma_{RI}$;
per-method incubation-time tolerance $\tau$; a timing base of 84 (method,
sample-tier) rows giving the viable window $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$; a
marker$\times$method fluorophore-compatibility matrix; a tissue table; and
tissue-RI references.

\noindent\textbf{Provenance.} Each timing row records a literature source key,
source URL, evidence locator, confidence level, and review status, giving an
audit trail from each parameter to its literature basis (Table~\ref{tab:kbprov}).
RI references cite their originating protocol (e.g., CUBIC RI${=}1.46$, Susaki
et al., \emph{Nat. Protoc.}). Timing tolerances are derived deterministically as
$\tau{=}0.2\times$ the median clearing time. Parameters are literature-bounded
and refined by expert review rather than exhaustively re-measured.

\noindent\textbf{Operationalization.} The Effectiveness terms read directly from
these bases: $S_{method}$ from tissue/method preference vectors (cosine);
$S_{label}$ from the fluorophore-compatibility matrix (hard zero on quenching);
$S_{trans}$ from RI references and $\sigma_{RI}$, gated to zero when a (method,
tier) pair is unsupported; and $S_{time}$ from $[t_{min},t_{max}]$ and $\tau$.
