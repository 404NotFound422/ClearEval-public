\begin{abstract}
Tissue Optical Clearing (TOC) enables non-destructive, high-resolution 3D
imaging, but protocol design requires compatible choices of clearing method,
labeling strategy, refractive-index condition, and incubation time. While LLMs
perform well on biomedical knowledge tasks, their ability to generate feasible
wet-lab protocols remains under-evaluated. We introduce ClearEval, a
database-grounded feasibility benchmark for LLM-generated TOC protocols.
ClearEval is built on a structured TOC feasibility database integrating 372
wet-lab experimental records, 400+ curated tissue-clearing literature chunks,
and expert-reviewed constraints on methods, tissues, markers, timing, and
compatibility. It evaluates 12 state-of-the-art LLMs through two tracks: 2{,}588
multiple-choice questions for static knowledge and 253 open-ended scenarios for
protocol generation. Generated protocols are scored by our Completeness,
Correctness, and Effectiveness (CCE) framework, which combines rubric-based
procedural assessment with database-grounded feasibility rules and formulas.
Models reach 80--91% accuracy on static knowledge, but performance drops on
executable protocol generation; the strongest model falls from 91.1% Knowledge
Index to a 64.1 Application Index. The main failures arise from executable
parameterization, especially matching fluorescent markers to required labeling
targets. ClearEval provides a depth-first feasibility benchmark that complements
breadth-oriented protocol benchmarks and supports pre-experimental evaluation in TOC and related wet-lab subdomains. Code and data:
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ClearEval}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Tissue Optical Clearing (TOC) renders biological tissues optically transparent
by reducing light scattering and refractive-index mismatch, enabling
non-destructive, high-resolution 3D imaging via light-sheet
microscopy~\cite{ueda2020tissue,chung2013clarity}. However, designing a TOC
protocol is not a single-answer task. Given a specimen, labeling target,
imaging goal, and laboratory constraint, experimenters must choose a feasible
combination of clearing method, labeling strategy, refractive-index condition,
incubation schedule, sample geometry, and operational constraints. Multiple
combinations may be acceptable, but one incompatibility---for example, a
quenched fluorophore, an unsuitable method for the sample scale, or an
unrealistic incubation time---can invalidate the protocol and irreversibly
damage the specimen.

Existing biomedical LLM benchmarks, including MedQA~\cite{jin2021disease},
MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}, and recent biomedical
extensions~\cite{zhang2025benchmarking,wang2025cardbiomedbench,feng2024mediq},
mainly evaluate knowledge retrieval, question answering, or text-based
reasoning. These settings measure whether models know biomedical facts, but
they do not directly test whether a generated wet-lab protocol is feasible
under experimental constraints. Likewise, semantic metrics such as
BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu} and general-purpose LLM
evaluators~\cite{liu2023geval,li2024survey} do not explicitly evaluate
method--label compatibility, refractive-index suitability, or timing
plausibility. This creates a gap between knowledge evaluation and
pre-experimental feasibility checking.

We introduce \textbf{ClearEval}, a database-grounded feasibility benchmark for
LLM-generated TOC protocols. ClearEval integrates 372 wet-lab experimental
records, 400+ curated tissue-clearing literature chunks, and expert-reviewed
TOC constraints into a structured feasibility database. This database supports
both task construction and protocol scoring. The benchmark contains 2{,}588
multiple-choice questions for static knowledge and 253 open-ended scenarios for
protocol generation. Generated protocols are evaluated by our
\textbf{CCE (Completeness, Correctness, Effectiveness)} framework, which
combines rubric-based procedural assessment with database-grounded rules and
formulas for experimental effectiveness.

Experiments on 12 state-of-the-art LLMs show that static knowledge performance
is close to saturation, whereas open-ended feasibility evaluation remains
discriminative. For example, GPT-5.2-Fast achieves 91.1% on the Knowledge
Index but only 64.1 on the Application Index
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results_total}). The radar plots further show that MCQ
profiles are tightly clustered, while OEQ-based CCE scores expose
model-specific execution failures. The most reliable failures arise from
executable parameterization, especially matching fluorescent markers to the
required labeling targets. Our contributions are: (1) a structured TOC
feasibility database integrating experimental records, literature evidence, and
expert constraints; (2) a dual-track benchmark and database-grounded CCE
evaluator for LLM-generated TOC protocols; and (3) an empirical analysis
showing that feasibility evaluation reveals execution-level failures hidden by
static knowledge tasks.



\section{Related Works}\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Scientific and biomedical LLM evaluation.}
The evaluation of scientific and biomedical LLMs has moved from general
knowledge tests toward more specialized biomedical and research-oriented tasks.
Benchmarks such as MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu},
MedQA~\cite{jin2021disease}, Med-PaLM-style
evaluations~\cite{singhal2023medpalm}, and recent biomedical
benchmarks~\cite{zhang2025benchmarking,wang2025cardbiomedbench,feng2024mediq}
measure knowledge retrieval, clinical reasoning, biomedical NLP, and
interactive question answering. These resources provide standardized evidence
for biomedical competence, but their outputs are usually closed-form answers,
short explanations, or task-specific labels rather than executable wet-lab
protocols.

\noindent\textbf{Biological protocol and wet-lab benchmarks.}
Recent benchmarks have extended LLM evaluation toward biological protocols and
laboratory workflows. BioProBench~\cite{bioprobench} studies biological
protocol understanding and reasoning across multiple subfields, including
protocol QA, step ordering, error correction, generation, and reasoning.
LAB-Bench~\cite{labbench} evaluates language models on biology research tasks,
including literature use, protocol planning, and data analysis. BixBench
~\cite{bixbench} considers multi-step computational-biology agent tasks. These
benchmarks characterize broad biological or research capabilities. ClearEval
focuses on a complementary setting: parameter-level feasibility assessment for
LLM-generated Tissue Optical Clearing protocols, where multiple method--marker
--time combinations may be acceptable and must be checked against explicit
experimental constraints.

\noindent\textbf{Automatic evaluation, TOC, and pre-experimental checking.}
Automatic evaluation methods, including text-overlap metrics such as
BLEU~\cite{papineni2002bleu} and LLM-based evaluators such as
G-Eval~\cite{liu2023geval}, enable scalable assessment of generated outputs.
Recent work has also analyzed systematic effects in LLM-as-a-judge
methods~\cite{li2024survey,zheng2023judging}. In ClearEval, LLM judgment is
used for rubric-defined procedural dimensions, while Effectiveness is computed
from a structured TOC feasibility database. TOC methods such as CLARITY, CUBIC,
iDISCO, uDISCO, and PEGASOS have enabled high-resolution 3D imaging across
tissues and organ scales~\cite{chung2013clarity,ueda2020tissue,
wang2025advances,richardson2025unlocking,mai2024tissue}. This literature
provides the basis for reasoning about method suitability, refractive-index
matching, labeling preservation, and clearing time. ClearEval organizes these
resources into a machine-readable feasibility checker for LLM-generated
protocols, which may also serve as a pre-experimental component for future
vertical scientific agents~\cite{boiko2023autonomous,hartung2025ai}.

\section{The ClearEval Benchmark}
ClearEval is organized around three linked components: a TOC feasibility
database, a dual-track task suite, and a scoring framework
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). The feasibility database converts experimental
records, literature evidence, and expert-reviewed TOC constraints into
machine-readable resources. The task suite uses these resources to construct
knowledge questions and open-ended protocol-design scenarios. The scoring
framework evaluates model outputs with a Knowledge Index ($I_K$) for MCQs and
an Application Index ($I_A$) for generated protocols.

\subsection{TOC Feasibility Database}
ClearEval formalizes TOC protocol constraints as a structured feasibility
database. The database integrates 372 wet-lab experimental records used to
support clearing-time intervals, 400+ curated tissue-clearing literature
chunks, and expert-reviewed constraints on tissues, molecular targets,
fluorescent markers, clearing-method properties, refractive-index conditions,
sample scales, and compatibility rules. These resources are encoded into seven
machine-readable knowledge bases, with inventory, provenance, and review
status documented in Appendix~\ref{app:curation}.

The database is designed to support feasibility assessment rather than
single-answer protocol matching. For method selection, each clearing method is
represented along six experimental axes: fluorescence/protein preservation, dye
permeability, clearing capacity, sample geometry, operational economy, and
biosafety. These axes provide a shared constraint space for comparing the
requirements of an experimental scenario with the properties of candidate
clearing strategies.

\subsection{Task Suite Construction}
ClearEval contains two task tracks. The Knowledge track evaluates static TOC
knowledge. Expert-authored templates are instantiated against the feasibility
database through controlled-vocabulary substitution, producing 2{,}588
multiple-choice questions: 533 named-entity questions, 1{,}350
domain-knowledge questions, and 705 task-scenario questions. Each question uses
an 8-option format, followed by deduplication and option shuffling.

The Application track evaluates open-ended TOC protocol generation. We define
60 expert-designed scientific missions across biological scales and combine
them with the feasibility database to construct constrained experimental
scenarios. These scenarios are further augmented with real wet-lab challenges
and quality-filtered to 253 open-ended tasks. Because TOC protocol design may
admit multiple feasible method--marker--time combinations, these tasks are not
graded by reference-text similarity. They are instead evaluated by the scoring
framework below.

\subsection{Scoring Framework}
ClearEval scores model outputs at two levels (Table~\ref{tab:metrics}). The
Knowledge Index ($I_K$) is the macro-averaged accuracy over MCQ categories. The
Application Index ($I_A$) evaluates open-ended generated protocols through three
dimensions: Completeness, Correctness, and Effectiveness:
\begin{equation}
I_A = \min(\text{Completeness}, \text{Correctness}, \text{Effectiveness}).
\end{equation}
This bottleneck formulation reflects wet-lab practice: a protocol can be
detailed and fluent, yet still fail if one essential component is missing,
inconsistent, or experimentally infeasible. Thus, CCE denotes the scoring
structure of the Application track, whereas ClearEval as a whole combines both
static knowledge evaluation and application-level feasibility assessment.

\paragraph{Completeness and Correctness.}
Completeness measures whether a generated protocol provides the information
needed to specify a TOC experiment. It includes Step Integrity, which checks
whether essential stages such as fixation or pre-treatment, labeling, clearing,
refractive-index matching, and imaging preparation are present when applicable,
and Parameter Detail, which checks whether key experimental parameters are
specified, including reagent or method choices, sample scale, incubation time,
temperature, concentration, labeling conditions, and imaging settings.

Correctness measures whether the specified protocol is internally consistent
and compatible with basic TOC principles. It includes Sequential Logic, which
checks whether operations follow a plausible wet-lab order; Consistency, which
checks for contradictions in sample scale, timing, or method choice; and
Physics Plausibility, which checks whether the protocol respects constraints
such as diffusion, tissue scale, refractive-index matching, and preservation of
the labeling signal.

Completeness and Correctness are evaluated by an LLM judge
under a fixed rubric, because they require interpretation of free-form
procedural text.

\paragraph{Effectiveness.}
Effectiveness evaluates whether the extracted experimental choices satisfy
TOC-specific feasibility constraints. Unlike Completeness and Correctness, it
is scored by deterministic, non-LLM checks that read from the TOC feasibility
database (Appendix~\ref{app:curation}). From each generated protocol, we
extract the clearing method, labeling target, fluorescent marker or labeling
strategy, refractive-index condition, and total processing time. These fields
are scored by four sub-metrics:
\begin{equation}
E_{score}
= S_{method} + S_{label} + S_{trans} + S_{time},
\end{equation}
with maxima scores $5$, $6$, $3$, and $3$, respectively.

\noindent\textbf{Method suitability ($S_{method}$, max $5$).}
Each clearing method has a six-dimensional capability vector
$\mathbf{v}_{ref}\in[0,1]^6$ over the experimental axes defined above. Each
scenario is parsed into a demand vector $\mathbf{v}_{pred}$ over the same
axes. Method suitability is computed as
\begin{equation}
S_{method}
= 5 \cdot \max\big(0,\cos(\mathbf{v}_{pred}, \mathbf{v}_{ref})\big).
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Label compatibility ($S_{label}$, max $6$).}
This metric evaluates whether the proposed labeling strategy is compatible
with the biological target and the selected clearing method:
\begin{equation}
S_{label}
= \min(S_{target}, S_{marker}, S_{method\text{-}marker}).
\end{equation}
$S_{target}$ checks whether the marker matches the required labeling target;
$S_{marker}$ checks marker--fluorophore compatibility; and
$S_{method\text{-}marker}$ checks fluorophore compatibility with the selected
clearing method using the quenching matrix. Each term is scored on $[0,6]$.
The minimum operator implements a hard feasibility rule: failure of any one
check makes the labeling strategy unusable.

\noindent\textbf{Transparency validity ($S_{trans}$, max $3$).}
This metric evaluates whether the refractive-index condition associated with
the selected method is compatible with the tissue and sample scale. The score
penalizes the gap between the tissue reference RI ($RI_{tissue}$) and the
method RI ($RI_{ref}$) using a Gaussian tolerance $\sigma_{RI}$:
\begin{equation}
S_{trans}
= 3 \cdot
\exp\left(
-\frac{(RI_{tissue}-RI_{ref})^2}{2\sigma_{RI}^2}
\right).
\end{equation}
$S_{trans}$ is set to $0$ when the selected method has no supported entry for
the requested sample scale in the timing database.

\noindent\textbf{Time efficiency ($S_{time}$, max $3$).}
The proposed total processing time is compared with an evidence-supported
window $[t_{min},t_{max}]$. These windows are derived from TOC literature,
372 wet-lab experimental records, and expert review. Let $\Delta t$ denote the
deviation from this window, with $\Delta t=0$ when the proposed time falls
inside the interval. Out-of-window timing is penalized by
\begin{equation}
S_{time}
= 3 \cdot
\exp\left(
-\frac{\Delta t^2}{2\tau^2}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is set deterministically to $0.2\times$ the median clearing time.

All timing windows, RI references, and $\sigma_{RI}/\tau$ tolerances are
traceable to literature evidence, wet-lab records, and expert review
(Appendix~\ref{app:curation}). The raw $E_{score}$, with maximum $17$, is
min--max normalized to $[0,100]$ before being combined with Completeness and
Correctness in the $I_A$ bottleneck.

\paragraph{Overall Expert Score.}
The final ClearEval Expert Score combines static knowledge and
application-level feasibility using the harmonic mean:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score} = \frac{2 I_K I_A}{I_K + I_A}.
\end{equation}
This aggregation penalizes models that perform well on TOC knowledge questions
but fail to generate experimentally feasible protocols.

\subsection{Main Results: The Knowledge-Execution Gap}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results_total} reveals a stark capability imbalance between
static recall and wet-lab execution: Knowledge indices cluster at $80$--$91$,
yet the bottleneck Application Index $I_A$ ranges from $64$ down to $18$. This
is visually highlighted in Fig.~\ref{fig:radar_chart}: the left panel shows all
models with uniformly high, tightly clustered Knowledge proficiency, whereas
Application Performance (right) is highly erratic. Models generally maintain
high procedural completeness ($79$ on average) but collapse on Correctness
($50$) and Effectiveness ($59$); for the three strongest models the binding
constraint is Effectiveness, for the other nine it is Correctness---though that
dimension is partly deflated by the weakly-calibrated $co\_chem$ sub-score
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}). The most reliable single failure is label suitability
($S_{label}$): models propose fluorescent markers that miss the scenario's
required labeling targets (Sec.~\ref{sec:diag}), exposing a severe
knowledge--execution gap.

\subsection{Calibration of the Automated Metrics}\label{sec:calib}
We validate the automated CCE scores against blinded expert consensus on 240
protocols (20 scenarios $\times$ 12 models). Because Pearson correlation alone
ignores absolute bias, we report, per dimension, Pearson $r$, Spearman $\rho$,
ICC(2,1), quadratic-weighted $\kappa$, and Krippendorff's $\alpha$
(Table~\ref{tab:calib}; full 14-row sub-dimension breakdown in
Appendix~\ref{app:judge}). The aggregate CCE dimensions align well with experts
(ICC $0.75$/$0.65$/$0.81$ for Completeness/Correctness/Effectiveness; Overall
$r=0.84$). Crucially, agreement is \emph{uneven} across the Effectiveness
sub-metrics: the empirically-grounded label-compatibility ($S_{label}$, ICC
$0.86$) and timing ($S_{time}$, ICC $0.82$) checks align strongly with experts,
and transparency ($S_{trans}$, ICC $0.58$) moderately, whereas the
cosine-similarity method-suitability score ($S_{method}$, ICC $0.04$, $r=0.11$)
is near-degenerate---it is therefore reported as a soft preference signal rather
than a calibrated score. Among the two LLM-judged dimensions, the weakest
agreement is on chemical correctness ($co\_chem$, ICC $0.32$). We thus restrict
LLM-as-judge to Completeness and Correctness---noting that $co\_chem$ is the
least reliable sub-score and reading the corresponding failure as suggestive
(Sec.~\ref{sec:diag})---and rely on the non-LLM physical checks for
Effectiveness, where the dominant failure ($S_{label}$) is also the
best-calibrated ($\mathrm{ICC}\,0.86$).

\subsection{Fine-Grained Diagnosis: Where Execution Fails}\label{sec:diag}
Averaging normalized sub-scores over all 253 scenarios and 12 models, the
\emph{reliably}-measured deficit concentrates in label suitability
(Fig.~\ref{fig:violin_limits}). \textbf{Label suitability} ($S_{label}$, mean
$0.23$) is a hard minimum over a marker--target match and two
fluorophore-compatibility checks, and $66\%$ of protocols score $0$. Decomposing
these zeros from the scorer's own breakdown shows the binding cause is
\textbf{marker--target mismatch}---the fluorescent markers models propose do not
match the scenario's required labeling targets---rather than fluorophore
quenching, which is the minority case. Because $S_{label}$ is our best-calibrated
Effectiveness check (ICC $0.86$, Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}), this is the most
trustworthy execution failure we observe. The nominally lower chemical-safety
score ($co\_chem$, mean $0.07$) requires care: inspecting the judge's rationales
shows it is dominated by penalties for the \emph{absence} of safety/handling
caveats (fume-hood use, solvent-compatible containers) that our generation prompt
never requested; given this and its weak calibration (ICC $0.32$), we treat
$co\_chem$ as suggestive rather than a capability measure. By contrast,
transparency / refractive-index matching ($S_{trans}$, $0.64$) and timing
($S_{time}$, $0.59$) are only moderately impaired---though $S_{time}$ is
sensitive to the width of the literature time windows, and individual estimates
can still be wildly off (occasionally exceeding 800 hours against a 0--200 hour
window). The cosine method-suitability score ($S_{method}$, $0.89$) appears high
but, being near-degenerate against experts (ICC $0.04$), is discounted. In short,
the most reliable wet-lab constraint these LLMs miss is matching fluorescent
markers to the scenario's required targets.

\subsection{Inference-Time Robustness Protocol}\label{sec:baselines}
A natural follow-up question is whether the knowledge--execution gap narrows
once standard inference-time techniques are applied, rather than being an
artifact of greedy decoding. To make this directly testable on ClearEval, we
release a turnkey harness that scores four recipes against the greedy default on
the full 253-scenario Application set: (i) \textbf{few-shot} (3 in-context
exemplars); (ii) \textbf{self-consistency} ($k{=}5$ samples at temperature
$0.7$, aggregated by method-majority then continuous-field median); (iii)
\textbf{KB-RAG}, prepending retrieved knowledge-base context (method properties
and compatibility records, with numeric RI/time targets withheld so the answer
key is never leaked); and (iv) \textbf{KB-RAG $+$ self-consistency}. Because
every recipe is scored by the same calibrated CCE pipeline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}), the bottleneck Application Index $I_A$---reported with
95\% confidence intervals over samples---is directly comparable across recipes
and against Table~\ref{tab:main_results_total}. We treat KB-RAG as an upper
bound on grounding benefit and leave full literature-RAG over an external corpus
to future work. The harness and the exact run protocol are released
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}); a complete multi-model sweep under paid inference is
ongoing and will be reported in an extended version. We deliberately do not
report partial numbers here to avoid drawing conclusions from an incomplete
sweep.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
Within TOC, ClearEval shows the bottleneck is not declarative retrieval but
executable parameterization---most reliably, matching fluorescent markers to the
required labeling targets, with refractive-index and timing errors
secondary. Whether this pattern generalizes across wet-lab subdomains is an open
question our single-subdomain design cannot settle.

\noindent\textbf{External validity.} ClearEval covers one subdomain (TOC). Our
claims are therefore about TOC protocol feasibility, not LLM scientific
competence in general. Generalizing the methodology requires re-curating
subdomain-specific physical knowledge bases (refractive indices, incubation
windows, compatibility matrices), which is non-trivial
(Appendix~\ref{app:curation}); we offer CCE as a transferable recipe rather than
a finished multi-domain benchmark.

\noindent\textbf{Complementarity.} Depth-first ClearEval and breadth-first
benchmarks such as BioProBench~\cite{bioprobench} are complementary: the former
contributes experiment-derived, parameter-level feasibility scoring within a
subdomain, the latter broad coverage. Together they give a fuller picture of LLM
protocol-design ability than either alone.

\noindent\textbf{Toward grounded agents.} For LLMs to become viable in
specialized wet-lab domains, they must internalize experimental
constraints---such as reagent diffusion scales---within their reasoning loops;
ClearEval can serve as a pre-experimental feasibility gate for such agents
before irreversible reagent commitment.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} ClearEval remains a static paradigm. Real-world
wet labs involve stochastic variables (reagent degradation, tissue
heterogeneity) that are hard to model in silico; our expert grounding is
literature-bounded and refined by expert review rather than exhaustively
re-measured; and the cosine method-suitability sub-score is weakly calibrated
(Sec.~\ref{sec:calib}). Future work should integrate dynamic hardware feedback
so agents iteratively optimize protocols from real-time signals.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented ClearEval, an expert-curated benchmark for TOC protocol design
that shifts evaluation from semantic similarity to empirically grounded
functional validation. By leveraging a dual-track pipeline and a data-driven
CCE framework, ClearEval exposes a significant knowledge-execution gap in
current LLMs on TOC protocol design. Our results indicate that while models
excel at knowledge recall, they struggle with the marker--target matching and
physical-feasibility constraints required for wet-lab execution. We anticipate
that ClearEval---and the transferable CCE recipe---will facilitate the
development of reliable scientific agents, starting with tissue-clearing
workflows.

\section{Knowledge-Base Curation and Expert Grounding}\label{app:curation}
\noindent\textbf{Expert panel and consensus.} The knowledge bases and the CCE
rubric were curated by a panel of tissue-clearing wet-lab experts together with
a knowledge-graph specialist. Application protocols were first scored blind
against the rubric and then reconciled by panel consensus; the released scores
are per-protocol consensus values.

\noindent\textbf{Knowledge bases.} Grounding is encoded in seven JSON knowledge
bases: reference refractive index for 18 clearing methods (CUBIC, ScaleS, MACS,
iDISCO+, FDISCO, SOLID, SeeDB2, PEGASOS, BoneClear, Ce3D, TDE, ClearT2, ClearSee,
EyeCi, FOCM, SWITCH, uDISCO, FlyClear); per-method RI tolerance $\sigma_{RI}$;
per-method incubation-time tolerance $\tau$; a timing base of 84 (method,
sample-tier) rows giving the viable window $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$; a
marker$\times$method fluorophore-compatibility matrix; a tissue table; and
tissue-RI references.

\noindent\textbf{Provenance.} Each timing row records a literature source key,
source URL, evidence locator, confidence level, and review status, giving an
audit trail from each parameter to its literature basis (Table~\ref{tab:kbprov}).
RI references cite their originating protocol (e.g., CUBIC RI${=}1.46$, Susaki
et al., \emph{Nat. Protoc.}). Timing tolerances are derived deterministically as
$\tau{=}0.2\times$ the median clearing time. Parameters are literature-bounded
and refined by expert review rather than exhaustively re-measured.

\noindent\textbf{Operationalization.} The Effectiveness terms read directly from
these bases: $S_{method}$ from tissue/method preference vectors (cosine);
$S_{label}$ from the fluorophore-compatibility matrix (hard zero on quenching);
$S_{trans}$ from RI references and $\sigma_{RI}$, gated to zero when a (method,
tier) pair is unsupported; and $S_{time}$ from $[t_{min},t_{max}]$ and $\tau$.
